Full English transcript

Stop one: the silhouette. Do not count every roof yet. Notice that the palace resembles a town of separate volumes rather than one building. Passages connect the chambers, while each group retains its own upper profile.

Stop two: the lower level. Look closely at the walls. The present building uses a modern structure and log cladding. It is not surviving seventeenth-century timber.

Stop three: the site. The original palace stood in the Sovereign's Court. The reconstruction was placed farther south, beyond the Golosov Ravine, so that the historical ensemble would not be occupied by the new building.

Stop four: the evidence. Measured records, drawings, images, and a nineteenth-century model survived the palace. Archaeological finds and museum collections informed individual details and interiors.

Conclusion. The present palace does not restore the old material or the old site. It makes the documented system of volumes visible at full scale. That is why its precise designation matters: a historical and artistic reconstruction of a lost palace.

A human story

The carver who preserved a palace at 1:40 scale

After the building disappeared, memory survived through manual work as well as texts and drawings.

Between 1865 and 1868, the carver D. A. Smirnov made a wooden model commissioned by Alexander II. It translated flat drawings into volume and made the system of terems and passages visible again.

The model is not a time machine or an independent eyewitness. It derives from surviving documents and represents one volumetric interpretation that must be compared with drawings, views, and archaeology.

Museum position

Critical position

A new work, not a returned monument
