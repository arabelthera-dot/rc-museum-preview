The painting and the painterHard to believe

Fact 1Why the ninth wave, exactly

An old sailors' belief: in a storm the waves come in groups, and the ninth is the tallest and deadliest. Every nation counted differently — the Greeks feared the third wave, the Romans the tenth; Russian sailors settled on the ninth. Oceanographers confirm half of it: waves really do travel in groups with one rising above the rest, but that one carries no fixed number.

Fact 2He read his own obituary

In the autumn of 1844 Aivazovsky's ship was caught in a storm in the Bay of Biscay. The vessel was presumed lost and St Petersburg newspapers printed the artist's obituary. He survived and painted the sea for another 56 years. The Ninth Wave came six years after that night.

Fact 3He would not sell to the Pope. He gave it away

Gregory XVI wanted to buy his painting Chaos for the Vatican. Aivazovsky refused to sell — and presented it as a gift. He received a gold medal, and Gogol joked: 'You have raised Chaos in the Vatican.'

Fact 4A painting every four days — for 60 years

Around 6,000 canvases in one lifetime. Do the maths: a new painting every three or four days, six decades without a pause. The last one he never finished — he died at his easel.

Fact 5Russia's most decorated artist

Twenty-four orders from Russia, France, Greece and Turkey. He threw the Turkish ones into the sea after the Armenian massacres of the 1890s, saying he regretted not one of them.

HistoryThe night they survived

Dawn. The night in which hell broke a ship apart is over. A handful of people cling to a broken mast: they survived the storm, but ahead of them rises the ninth wave — by sailors' belief the deadliest of all. And yet the first thing a viewer sees on this three-metre canvas is not the wave. It is the sun.

The author of this sea was Hovhannes, son of a ruined Armenian merchant from Feodosia. As a boy he drew with charcoal on the town fences — and instead of a whipping he was sent to school: the story goes that the governor himself noticed the drawings. Then came the gymnasium, the Academy of Arts in St Petersburg, and by thirty-three European fame under the name Ivan Aivazovsky.

The strangest thing about the world's foremost marine painter: he never painted the sea from life. 'The movements of the living elements are elusive for the brush,' he said. He would watch a storm for hours, commit it to memory, then retreat to a studio whose windows faced a blank wall — so that memory would work, not the eyes. The Ninth Wave, two metres by three, was painted this way in eleven days. This is not a legend but a documented fact.

Above all, he knew that night first-hand. Six years before the painting, in 1844, his ship was caught in a storm in the Bay of Biscay. The vessel was presumed lost and the newspapers printed his obituary. 'Fear did not suppress in me the ability to keep the impression of the storm in my memory, like a marvellous living picture,' the artist recalled. The Ninth Wave is that very night, seen from within and defeated.

Emperor Nicholas I bought the painting for the Hermitage gallery. In 1897 it was transferred to the newly founded Russian Museum and became one of its first exhibits when it opened in 1898. It lives there still; in 2018 it travelled to Japan and became the centrepiece of the exhibition.

There is a bitter note in this story. By the end of the century younger painters and critics dismissed Aivazovsky's manner as outdated showmanship. He died in 1900 at his easel — and ten years later Russian art turned to the avant-garde, filing his seas away as 'salon' work for half a century. Time judged otherwise: today The Ninth Wave is one of the most recognisable Russian paintings in the world, and in Russian the phrase itself has come to mean the highest point of an ordeal.

The artist in one paragraph. Ivan Konstantinovich Aivazovsky (1817–1900). First painter to the Russian Naval Staff, with the right to wear naval uniform. Honorary citizen of Feodosia, to which he gave a picture gallery that now bears his name. Around six thousand works. He died at his easel with his last painting unfinished.

Close readingFor those who look closely

Why the water glows from within

The secret is not white paint but patience. Aivazovsky worked in glazes, laying the thinnest transparent layers one over another, each almost like glass. Light passes through them, reflects off the pale ground and returns to the viewer from inside the canvas. That is why his wave crest is not 'painted white' but genuinely glows: the paint works like stained glass. Zoom into the crest and you will find almost no pure white there.

Composition: everything rests on a single point

The painting looks like chaos but is built by academic rule. The crest of the ninth wave sits exactly where the canvas's structural lines cross, while the sun and the mast with its survivors lie on one diagonal — the eye travels from the dying to the light and back, unable to stop. Hence the sense of motion: the wave is not painted moving, it is composed that way.

A Russian marine painter in the European league

In Rome in 1842 Aivazovsky met J. M. W. Turner, Europe's leading marine painter. The Englishman admired the precision with which the Russian rendered transparent water. Both followed the same road: a bright palette, light as the protagonist, the elements instead of a plot. The difference is that Turner dissolved form in light, while Aivazovsky never let light loose from form — his storm stays a recognisable storm and his people stay people. The Ninth Wave was painted in 1850, when its author was thirty-three.

The journey of the canvas

Nicholas I bought the painting at once, for the Hermitage. In 1897 it became one of the first acquisitions of the newly created Russian Museum of Emperor Alexander III, where it hangs to this day. In a century and a half the canvas has never left St Petersburg for long: too large and too fragile for distant journeys.
