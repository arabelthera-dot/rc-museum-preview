Chain of choices

Three roads — and he chose the deadly one

Surrender, retreat, keep quiet — in his position that would have been common sense. Kolovrat followed each of the three roads to its end — and rejected them all.

He could have stayedChernigov: a roof, bread, a tomorrow

With Prince Ingvar he was safe. Nobody expected a commander without a city to return to the ashes. Had he stayed, he would have lived a life nobody remembers.

He could have waitedThe horde will leave — and life goes on

An invasion passes like a fire: sit it out behind walls, then come back and rebuild. Many did so. So did almost everyone whose name has not come down to us.

He could have servedThe strong khan, as one serves the victor

Batu knew how to reward those who bowed. Many who survived 1237 later served the horde — and lived well fed. That was the road of survival.

Kolovrat rejected all three roads. He chose a fourth that no one had reckoned with: to chase down an army a hundred times stronger and give it battle.
Source

What the “Tale” says

The only written witness to Kolovrat is “The Tale of the Destruction of Ryazan by Batu”, an Old Russian text of the 13th century that survives in 16th-century copies. Below are its words, abridged.

“The Tale of the Destruction of Ryazan by Batu” · 13th century

“…and he came to the land of Ryazan and saw it deserted: the towns destroyed, the churches burned, the people slain. And he rushed in pursuit of the godless khan, to drink the one cup of death together with his lords».

“The Tatars began to fight him, thinking that the dead had risen. And the strong regiments barely overcame him. And they brought five wounded men to Batu… And Batu asked them: ‘Who are you?’ And they answered: ‘We were sent to honour you, mighty khan.’”

“And Batu said, looking at his body: ‘O Evpaty Kolovrat! You have treated me well with your small band… Had such a man served me, I would hold him close to my heart.’ And he gave the body to his men and let the captives go.”

Primary source — “The Tale of the Destruction of Ryazan by Batu”, ed. D. S. Likhachev, “Military Tales of Ancient Rus”.
Paradox in numbers

Hard to believe

Fact 1A small band against an army that took cities

According to the “Tale”, Kolovrat had about 1,700 men. Against them stood a horde that in one campaign took Ryazan and half of North-Eastern Rus. The numbers do not add up — and that is exactly why the name survived: the battle was not for victory but for something else.

Fact 2“As if the dead had risen”

Seeing how the band fought, the Tatars decided that the slain people of Ryazan had risen against them. Fear of the dead is the very detail the “Tale” was written for: an enemy who does not fear death is more frightening than death.

Fact 3He was killed like a fortress

They could not strike him down in battle — and, according to the “Tale”, overcame him with “a multitude of siege engines”: stone-throwing machines, weapons made for walls. A man had to be taken like a city.

Fact 4The enemy released his men

This is not how one treats the despised: according to the “Tale”, Batu gave up the body and released the captives. Even in the legend the enemy admits he faced not a bandit but a warrior worth remembering.

The deed

A battle that could not be won

Returning from Chernigov to the ashes of Ryazan, Kolovrat found neither prince nor city — only land the horde had crossed. He gathered those who survived and caught up with Batu in the Suzdal land. He struck suddenly, at the camps, in the back of an army that never expected anyone to follow it.

The fighting was fiercer than anything the horde had seen. Batu sent against Kolovrat his brother-in-law, the strongman Khostovrul, who promised to bring the commander back alive. In single combat Kolovrat cut him in two, down to the saddle.

No one could take him alive. And then, as the “Tale” says, he was killed in a way no man is killed: siege engines built to smash walls were turned on his band. Kolovrat died — but the horde that had taken dozens of cities remembered this fight.

Batu, according to the legend, looked at the dead man and spoke words that have come down to us across seven centuries: had such a man served me, I would hold him close to my very heart.

What really happened here and what the legend added, historians still argue. But the essence is one, and it does not depend on numbers or details: a man who had lost everything chose not to surrender. Not for victory — there was no victory. To remain himself.

Cost and memory

Fact and myth — honestly

Kolovrat is a figure of the “Tale”. We say plainly what is history here and what is legend.

Expand — what is known and what is not

The only source is the “Tale”

There are no other mentions of Kolovrat in 13th-century chronicles. Everything we know about him comes from “The Tale of the Destruction of Ryazan by Batu”. Medievalists — Dmitry Likhachev, Anton Gorsky — consider the episode semi-legendary: it is probably based on a lost epic song or a local Ryazan tradition.

What is probably literature

The number “1,700”, death by siege engines, the duel with Khostovrul — these are motifs the “Tale” took from the bookish tradition (the Life of Hypatius of Gangra and military tales). They are not necessarily invented wholesale, but they came from literature, not from a chronicle.

What remains true in any case

The invasion of 1237 and the destruction of Ryazan are historical fact. And the image of a man who, after his city perished, did not surrender but went into a battle he was bound to lose — this image has outlived both chronicle and legend. That is exactly why it comes first in the museum “Russians Do Not Surrender”.

Your choice

What would you have done?

The year 1237. You are a commander, your city is burned, your prince is dead. You are safe, in someone else’s city. The horde is leaving. What do you do?

I stay in Chernigov — alive, with work to do, waiting out the disaster
I wait for the horde to leave — and go back to rebuild my home
I gather a small band and chase the horde — to give battle
Here is what he did. Kolovrat gathered those who survived — a “small band” — caught up with Batu in the Suzdal land and struck at the camps. He could not win the battle. But it was this choice, not the outcome of the fight, that made his name remembered seven centuries later.
